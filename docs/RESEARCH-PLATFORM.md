# One App For Everything — Platform Research & Verdict

**Date:** September 2026
**Question:** Expand from bedtime stories into rhymes, crafts, colouring, alphabet and words — one comprehensive app per household, CoComelon-style.

**Verdict:** The instinct is right and the positioning is strong. But three things in the framing will sink it if taken literally, and one of them is the CoComelon comparison itself.

---

## 1. The CoComelon model is the wrong half

Moonbug (CoComelon, Blippi) did **£253M revenue in FY2023**, growing at an **86% CAGR**, and sold for **~$3B**. Real business. But look at where the money comes from:

> **Advertising, licensing and merchandising.**

CoComelon is *free* on YouTube. The videos are not the product — they are the **distribution engine** that builds the IP, and the IP monetises through toys, ad revenue and 100+ licensing deals. Moonbug bought CoComelon and Blippi for $120M in 2020 and turned them into a licensing machine.

"The same approach but in the app space" inverts the model. You would be **putting behind a paywall the exact thing they give away free**, and you have none of the machinery — no ad inventory, no retail shelf, no licensing team — that the giveaway exists to feed.

**What actually transfers:** own your characters and your songs. Lumi and the original rhymes are the asset. That part is worth copying, and it is the reason to write original rhymes rather than only recycling traditional ones.

**What does not transfer:** the funnel, the economics, and the scale.

## 2. Your biggest competitor in "learning" is free, excellent, and Stanford-backed

**Khan Academy Kids is completely free, no ads, no locked content**, developed with the Stanford Graduate School of Education, covering reading, writing, maths and social-emotional development.

The paid tier of the market sits above it: **ABCmouse $12.99/mo** (10,000+ activities, up to 3 child profiles), **Lingokids $9.99/mo or $59.99/yr**.

So: you cannot win the "learning breadth" fight. Khan gives away more curriculum than you will build, for nothing, with better credentials. Any pitch that leads with "we also teach the alphabet" loses on contact.

**Therefore learning must be a supporting pillar, not the headline.** It exists so parents feel the app is nourishing. It is not the reason they buy.

## 3. Breadth is how kids' apps die

ABCmouse's most consistent complaint is a sprawling, dated interface — the natural end state of 10,000 activities. "One app for everything" reliably becomes sixth-best at six things, and parents delete the app that is mediocre at the one thing they opened it for.

The version of this that works is not *more surface area*. It is **one spine that the pillars hang off**, so that breadth feels like depth.

## 4. The contradiction you need to resolve

v1's differentiator was the strongest thing in the whole product:

> The only children's app whose success metric is that the session **ends**.

"Keep the kids engaged" is the precise opposite. And the ground has moved underneath engagement-maximising kids' apps:

The **AAP's 2026 policy statement** (*Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents*) **stopped leading with a daily hour limit** and now asks three questions instead: what is on the screen, who is watching with them, and **what the screen time is displacing** — sleep, play, connection. Research consistently separates the two modes: **passive viewing correlates with attention problems and language delays; interactive, co-viewed media does not, and sometimes benefits.**

An app that maximises engagement for under-11s in 2026 is swimming against both the pediatric consensus and the thing parents already feel guilty about.

### The resolution: build the day, not the feed

**One app that knows what time it is.** This is the spine, and it is the thing no competitor has.

| Mode | When | What it is | Engagement posture |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wake** | Morning | Rhymes, movement, counting, a song to get dressed to | Bright, loud, active. Engagement is *good* here. |
| **Play** | Daytime | Learn (phonics), Create (colouring, crafts), rhyme games | Interactive and co-played. Never passive. |
| **Wind-down** | Evening | The sleep gradient, the bedtime story, Lumi's eyes closing | Engagement is the **enemy**. The app dims and ends. |

This gets you everything at once:
- It is genuinely **one app for everything**, which is the selling point you want.
- It preserves **"the app that ends the session"**, which is the differentiator you already have.
- It answers the AAP's actual question — *what is this displacing?* — with "nothing; it hands the evening back."
- It gives parents a **ritual**, which is far stickier than a content library.

Marketing line: **"It knows when to stop."**

## 5. Rhymes: the licensing picture is permissive

- **Lyrics published before 1928 are public domain in the US.** Hey Diddle Diddle, Hickory Dickory Dock, Humpty Dumpty, Twinkle Twinkle (Jane Taylor, 1806), Mary Had a Little Lamb (Hale, 1830), Row Row Row Your Boat (1852) — all free to use.
- **But specific recordings and arrangements are separately copyrighted.** CoComelon's versions are protected. Anything written in the 20th century is likely still under copyright.

So the rule is simple and costs nothing: **use traditional words freely, never copy an arrangement, and generate all audio ourselves.** We already narrate on-device, so there is no recording to license and no per-character cost.

And then the better move: **write our own.** Original rhymes are the ownable asset, they can carry the child's name, and they tie to the eighteen worlds. That is the half of the CoComelon model that does transfer.

### What makes a rhyme actually stick — the evidence

This is a solved problem, and the findings are specific enough to build to:

1. **Trochaic metre (DUM-da).** Children grasp trochees earliest, because caregiver speech and names are trochaic. It produces the marching rhythm that makes a rhyme recitable.
2. **Rhyme words at phrase-final position.** Rhymes in songs get a measurable *perceptual boost* from landing at line end in a predictable rhythmic context. Put the rhyme on the beat, at the end, always.
3. **Predictability is the pleasure.** Children can predict the next *sound* without knowing the meaning — that is the game they are playing.
4. **A refrain.** A line that returns lets a child join in before they have learned the whole thing.
5. **A dropped final word (cloze).** Omitting the rhyme word and letting the child supply it is the single strongest participation mechanic available.
6. **Actions.** Clapping or tapping the rhythm reinforces the metrical pattern and aids memorisation.
7. **Nonsense and silliness.** Builds humour, which builds resilience and self-esteem.
8. **Cumulative structure and counting.** A memory ladder the child can climb.
9. **Short.** Four to eight lines for the youngest band.

Why it is worth doing well: **nursery rhyme knowledge is one of the strongest predictors of later reading and spelling**, holding up across social background, IQ and starting phonological skill. Rhyme is the on-ramp to phonological awareness, which is the on-ramp to reading. This pillar is not filler — it is the most evidence-backed thing in the entire app.

## 6. Colouring: the research says print beats screen

Occupational therapists are blunt about this. Holding a crayon builds the **pincer grasp**, grip strength and pressure control — the precursors to handwriting. **Tapping a screen to flood-fill a shape bypasses all of it.** Printed pages also hold attention far longer (15–20 minutes of continuous focus) with no flashing screen.

The honest conclusion is inconvenient for an app, and is therefore the opportunity:

> **Make the colouring feature print-first.** The app generates a personalised line-art page — the child's name, their companion, the world from last night's story — and the primary action is **Print**. On-screen colouring stays as the travel and waiting-room fallback.

A feature whose main button takes the child *off* the screen is the single most parent-trusted thing you can ship, it is consistent with "it knows when to stop", and no competitor will copy it because it reads as self-sabotage. It isn't. It's the moat.

## 7. Pricing: do not charge per child

The market has already answered this. **ABCmouse includes up to three child profiles in its base subscription.** Per-child and per-device pricing is what *parental-control* apps do, and it is a known source of resentment — a family with three children pays two to three times the single price.

Charging per kid would be a differentiator in the wrong direction. Recommended:

| | Price | Children | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Starlight** | $6.99 once | 1 | The existing offer, unchanged. |
| **Starlight Family** | $12.99 once | up to 4 | The default. One price, whole household. |
| Wish Sparks | $2.99 / 20 | shared | The only metered thing. Pooled across the family. |

Still one-time, still no subscription, still no ads — but the *library* is now four pillars deep, which is what justifies $12.99 where $6.99 bought stories alone. Multi-child is a **feature of the purchase, not a multiplier on it.**

## 8. The honest risk: the bottleneck moves from code to content

The story engine works because prose composes from lexicons. Rhymes, colouring pages, phonics sequences and crafts are each **authored artefacts**. Moonbug has animators and a studio. This is a content-operations problem, not an engineering one, and it is the thing most likely to stall the app.

Mitigations, in order of leverage:
1. **Templates that personalise**, so one authored artefact serves every child (already how stories work; rhymes now carry `{child}` and `{companion}` slots the same way).
2. **Public domain as ballast** — traditional rhymes, NASA/NOAA/Smithsonian imagery — so original work is spent only where it is the differentiator.
3. **Print-first crafts**, because a line-art SVG is enormously cheaper to author than an animation.
4. **Ship four pillars well before adding a fifth.** Crafts beyond colouring, music and cartoons are v3, not v2.

## 9. Verdict

| Dimension | Grade | Note |
|---|---|---|
| "One app, one household" positioning | **A** | Genuinely what parents want. Real selling point. |
| Rhymes as a pillar | **A** | Best evidence base in the app; licensing is free; original IP is ownable. |
| The day-arc spine | **A** | Resolves the engagement contradiction and is unique. |
| Colouring, print-first | **A−** | Pediatrically defensible and uncopyable for positioning reasons. |
| Learning as a pillar | **C+** | Fine as support. Fatal as the headline — Khan is free and better. |
| "CoComelon in app space" | **D** | Wrong half of the model. Own the characters, not the funnel. |
| Per-child pricing | **D** | Market has settled on household-flat. Do not fight it. |
| Content-ops risk | **C** | The real bottleneck. Templates and public domain are the mitigation. |

**Build the day, not the feed. Own the characters. Let the colouring page leave the screen.**
